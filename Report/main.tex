\begin{abstract}
Galaxy groups are the fundamental bound units of large-scale structure, yet their detection traditionally relies on a linking length calibrated against N-body simulations or adopted from prior surveys. We present an unsupervised cylindrical Friends-of-Friends (FoF) algorithm that derives its transverse linking length entirely from network topology, without physical calibration. Applied to a volume-limited sample of 75,042 galaxies from SDSS DR10 ($0.02 \leq z \leq 0.06$), a percolation scan over the transverse linking length $r_\perp$ identifies two topological markers: the susceptibility peak ($r_{\rm critical}^\perp = 1.34\,h^{-1}$ Mpc), which locates the percolation phase transition, and the fragmentation maximum ($r_{\rm groups}^\perp = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc), which locates the group scale. The sub-critical inequality $r_{\rm groups}/r_{\rm critical} \approx 0.40$ confirms a genuinely group-scale linking length. Validated against the Tempel et al. (2014) reference catalog, the detected groups achieve an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.807, Normalized Mutual Information of 0.983, and pair recall of 0.982. The inferred dimensionless linking parameter $b = 0.144$ is consistent with the classical FoF literature. Completeness exceeds 98\% at all richness scales; purity declines from 82\% at $N=2$ to 75\% at $N>50$, a predictable signature of the fixed line-of-sight stretch factor.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\rhostart{G}alaxy groups, gravitationally bound systems of $2$–$\sim\!100$ members, are the most common environment for galaxies in the universe and the fundamental node of the cosmic web \cite{tempel2014}. Their identification from spectroscopic redshift surveys relies on the Friends-of-Friends (FoF) algorithm \cite{huchra1982, davis1985}: link every pair of galaxies within a chosen linking length $\ell$; the connected components of the resulting graph are the groups. The method is structurally elegant — it maps a physical criterion (spatial proximity) directly onto a graph-theoretic object (connected components) — yet its central parameter, the dimensionless linking length $b = \ell / \bar{d}$, has traditionally required calibration against N-body simulations \cite{berlind2002, eke2004} or adoption from prior surveys \cite{tempel2014}.

The core question of this project is whether the galaxy spatial network itself encodes the group scale — whether a purely topological analysis of the graph, without any external calibration, can identify the physically correct linking length.

Percolation theory provides the natural framework \cite{newman2010, stauffer1994}. In a random geometric graph, connectivity transitions sharply at a critical linking length $r_c$: below $r_c$ the network fragments into isolated finite clusters, above $r_c$ a single giant connected component (GCC) percolates across the system. The susceptibility $\chi$ — the mean-square size of finite components, excluding the GCC — diverges at $r_c$ and acts as the order parameter of the transition. Below $r_c$, the finite components are precisely the structures we call galaxy groups. Above $r_c$, they merge into clusters, filaments, and superclusters.

Two topological observables are therefore available as unsupervised estimators of the group scale. The susceptibility peak locates the percolation threshold $r_{\rm critical}$, the hard upper bound above which group finding is no longer meaningful. The fragmentation count $N_{\rm groups}(r_\perp)$ — the number of finite components in the sub-GCC size range — peaks at the linking length where the network is most fragmented into distinct, finite groups. We identify this peak as the group scale $r_{\rm groups}$. The sub-critical inequality
\begin{equation}
    r_{\rm groups} < r_{\rm critical}
\end{equation}
is the defining property of the method and must be verified explicitly for every sample.

Redshift-space distortions require an anisotropic (cylindrical) FoF. Virialized galaxies have peculiar velocities of $\sim 300$–$500$ km/s that displace them by several Mpc along the line of sight (Fingers of God effect), while their true transverse extent is $\sim 0.5\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. We follow Tempel et al. \cite{tempel2014} in using a fixed stretch factor $S = r_\parallel / r_\perp = 10$.

Throughout this work, we use the Tempel et al. (2014) SDSS DR10 group catalog \cite{tempel2014} as both the data source and the validation reference. Galaxy positions, redshifts, and magnitudes come from their catalog; their group assignments serve as ground truth for validation. The linking length is derived entirely from the topology of the network we construct — it is never read from their paper.

\section{Data}

\subsection{Source catalog}

We query the Tempel et al. (2014) catalog from VizieR \cite{tempel2014}, table \inlinecode{J/A+A/566/A1/galaxies}. This table provides, per galaxy: sky coordinates $(\alpha, \delta)$, observed redshift $z_{\rm obs}$, apparent $r$-band magnitude $m$, and Tempel's group membership labels (\inlinecode{GroupID}, \inlinecode{Ngal}). The group labels are retained only for post-hoc validation and never enter the network construction.

\subsection{Cosmology}

We adopt a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology with $H_0 = 100\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ and $\Omega_m = 0.27$. All distances are in $h^{-1}$ Mpc by construction. Comoving distances are computed via \inlinecode{astropy.cosmology.FlatLambdaCDM}.

\subsection{Volume-limited sample}

SDSS is a flux-limited survey: at a fixed apparent magnitude limit, faint galaxies are only visible at low redshift, making the raw catalog inhomogeneous in depth. We apply two cuts to produce a uniform volume-limited sample.

\textbf{Redshift slice.} We retain galaxies with $0.02 \leq z \leq 0.06$. The lower bound avoids local peculiar-velocity contamination; the upper bound keeps the comoving volume compact enough that a single linking length is physically meaningful across the full depth. Extending to higher redshift would require a redshift-dependent linking length and luminosity evolution corrections, both of which we set aside for this project.

\textbf{Absolute magnitude cut.} We define the absolute magnitude limit at $z_{\max}$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:maglim}
    M_{\rm lim} = m_{\rm lim} - \mu(z_{\max}),
\end{equation}
where $\mu$ is the distance modulus and $m_{\rm lim} = 17.77$ (SDSS Main Galaxy Sample). Galaxies fainter than $M_{\rm lim}$ are discarded at all redshifts in the slice, ensuring uniform completeness across the volume. After both cuts the Main Galaxy Sample contains $N = 75{,}042$ galaxies, reduced from a raw catalog of 588,193.

\subsection{Coordinate conversion}

Each galaxy is converted from equatorial coordinates $(\alpha, \delta, d_{\rm com})$ to Cartesian $h^{-1}$ Mpc:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cartesian}
    \mathbf{r} = d_{\rm com}\!\left(\cos\delta\cos\alpha,\; \cos\delta\sin\alpha,\; \sin\delta\right),
\end{equation}
where $\alpha \equiv$ \inlinecode{ra} and $\delta \equiv$ \inlinecode{dec}. With Earth at the origin, the unit line-of-sight vector $\hat{r}_i = \mathbf{r}_i / |\mathbf{r}_i|$ is used in the cylindrical linking criterion.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Cylindrical linking criterion}

A pair $(i, j)$ is linked if it satisfies a two-condition cylindrical criterion:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cylinder}
    d_\perp \leq r_\perp \quad \text{and} \quad d_\parallel \leq r_\parallel = S \cdot r_\perp,
\end{equation}
with $S = 10$ following Tempel et al. \cite{tempel2014}. The perpendicular and parallel separations are decomposed along the midpoint line-of-sight direction $\hat{\ell}_{ij} = (\mathbf{r}_i + \mathbf{r}_j)/|\mathbf{r}_i + \mathbf{r}_j|$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
    d_\parallel = \left|(\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j) \cdot \hat{\ell}_{ij}\right|, \quad
    d_\perp = \sqrt{|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|^2 - d_\parallel^2}.
\end{equation}

Candidate pairs within the bounding sphere $r_{\max} = \sqrt{r_\perp^2 + r_\parallel^2}$ are found efficiently via a KD-tree on the Cartesian coordinates (scipy \inlinecode{KDTree}), and then filtered precisely by \equref{eq:cylinder}.

\subsection{Percolation scan}

We sweep $r_\perp$ from 0.1 to 2.5 $h^{-1}$ Mpc in steps of 0.02 $h^{-1}$ Mpc, and at each step build the cylindrical graph and compute two topological observables simultaneously.

\textbf{Susceptibility.} The percolation order parameter is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chi}
    \chi(r_\perp) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{s \neq {\rm GCC}} s^2\, n_s,
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over all finite components (excluding the GCC), $s$ is component size, $n_s$ is the count of components of size $s$, and $N$ is the total galaxy count. $\chi$ peaks at the percolation threshold $r_{\rm critical}$, the linking length at which the GCC first percolates across the system.

\textbf{Fragmentation count.} We count finite components of size $2 \leq s < |{\rm GCC}|$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ngroups}
    N_{\rm groups}(r_\perp) = \left|\{c \in G_{r_\perp} \mid 2 \leq |c| < |{\rm GCC}|\}\right|.
\end{equation}
This counts components that are neither isolated singletons nor the GCC. Because small structures vastly outnumber rich clusters, $N_{\rm groups}$ is unimodal in the sub-critical regime and its maximum is unambiguous. The peak marks the linking length at which the network is most fragmented into distinct, finite groups — just before they begin merging into the percolating structure. We call this $r_{\rm groups}$ and use it as the working linking length $r_{\rm link}$.

\figref{fig:phase_transition} shows both observables for our sample. The susceptibility peak ($r_{\rm critical} = 1.34\,h^{-1}$ Mpc) and the fragmentation maximum ($r_{\rm groups} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc) are well-separated, confirming \equref{eq:inequality}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:inequality}
    r_{\rm groups}^\perp = 0.54 \;<\; 1.34 = r_{\rm critical}^\perp \quad [h^{-1}{\rm\,Mpc}].
\end{equation}

\subsection{GCC diagnostic}

\figref{fig:gcc_diagnostic} shows the GCC fraction as a function of $r_\perp$ and its numerical derivative. The maximum gradient, a classical heuristic for the percolation threshold \cite{newman2010}, lies at $r \approx 1.26\,h^{-1}$ Mpc — slightly below the susceptibility peak, consistent with finite-size effects in a bounded survey volume. All three markers (maximum fragmentation, susceptibility peak, maximum gradient) are well-separated, confirming that they correspond to distinct physical regimes: groups, onset of clustering, and the supercluster network.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \placefig{figures/fig_gcc_diagnostic.pdf}
    \caption{GCC diagnostic. Top: GCC coverage as a function of $r_\perp$, with vertical markers. Bottom: derivative $d({\rm GCC})/dr_\perp$. The maximum gradient (orange) lies near the susceptibility peak (blue), both of which correspond to the cluster/supercluster scale.}
    \label{fig:gcc_diagnostic}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Linking parameter $b$}

As a post-hoc sanity check, we compute the dimensionless linking parameter $b \equiv r_{\rm link}/\bar{d}$, where $\bar{d} = n^{-1/3}$ is the mean inter-galaxy spacing and $n = N/V_{\rm shell}$ is the number density. The shell volume accounts for the SDSS footprint:
\begin{equation}
    V_{\rm shell} = \frac{4\pi}{3}(d_{\max}^3 - d_{\min}^3)\,f_{\rm sky}, \quad f_{\rm sky} = \frac{7221}{41252.96},
\end{equation}
where $f_{\rm sky}$ corresponds to the SDSS Main Galaxy Sample coverage of $\sim 7221\,{\rm deg}^2$ \cite{tempel2014}. This yields:
\begin{equation}
    b = 0.144,
\end{equation}
within the classical FoF range $b \in [0.14, 0.20]$ \cite{davis1985, berlind2002} and $\sim 25\%$ above Tempel's redshift-dependent value $b \approx 0.113$–$0.115$ for our redshift slice \cite{tempel2014}. This parameter is not used as an input — it is computed only to place the result in the context of prior work.

\subsection{Final network}

The final network $G$ is built by running the cylindrical FoF once at $r_\perp = r_{\rm groups} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. Connected components of $G$ are the galaxy groups by definition. Each galaxy receives a \inlinecode{Detected\_GroupID} label, including singletons. The network properties are summarised in \tabref{tab:topology}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Phase-transition figure}

The ratio $r_{\rm groups}/r_{\rm critical} = 0.54/1.34 \approx 0.40$ characterises the network's position in the percolation phase diagram. A ratio of 0.40 places the group scale well within the sub-critical regime — approximately at the onset of local clustering, before the connectivity transitions to global percolation. The fragmentation count reaches its maximum of $\sim\!15{,}000$ distinct groups at $r_{\rm groups}$, then falls rapidly as groups merge.

\section{Validation \& Assessment}

\subsection{Completeness and purity by richness}

For each Tempel group with $N \geq 2$, we identify the detected component with the largest membership overlap and compute:
\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{|T \cap D|}{|T|}, \qquad P = \frac{|T \cap D|}{|D|},
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the set of Tempel members and $D$ is the best-matching detected group. Completeness is the fraction of a true group recovered; purity is the fraction of the detected group that genuinely belongs to it. \tabref{tab:cp_richness} reports mean completeness and purity in five richness bins.

\section{Discussion}

The central result is that the maximum fragmentation criterion — derived entirely from the topology of the galaxy network — selects a linking length that closely reproduces a carefully calibrated observational catalog. The inferred $b = 0.144$ is within the classical FoF range and only $\sim 25\%$ above Tempel's $b \approx 0.113$, a difference consistent with the mild over-merging visible in \figref{fig:richness_dist}. That the method recovers the correct scale without reading any parameter from the literature suggests that the group scale is genuinely encoded in the network topology, not merely recoverable from it.

The high completeness ($>98\%$) across all richness bins and the stable redshift performance confirm two things. First, the cylindrical criterion is generous enough in the transverse direction to capture nearly all members of every group. Second, the fixed $r_\perp$ does not introduce a systematic redshift-dependent bias within the narrow slice, validating the volume-limited selection. These two results together indicate that the method is self-consistent within its domain of applicability.

The declining purity at high $N$ is a known, predictable limitation of a fixed stretch factor. The velocity dispersion of a virialized system scales as $\sigma \propto M^{1/3}$: a group with $N = 100$ members has a velocity spread $\sim 3$–$5\times$ larger than a pair, and a fixed $r_\parallel = S \cdot r_\perp$ merges it with neighboring structures along the line of sight. The clean resolution is a 2D joint scan over $(r_\perp, S)$: find the maximum fragmentation peak in the full two-parameter space. This approach is fully unsupervised, as it makes no assumption about what the stretch factor should be. Implementing this scan is the natural next step for this project.

The choice of $S = 10$, adopted from Tempel et al. \cite{tempel2014}, is the only external input to the current algorithm. In a strictly unsupervised implementation it should be either derived from the velocity dispersion of the detected components themselves, or jointly optimised with $r_\perp$ in the 2D scan.

The network topology results are informative independently of the validation against Tempel. The $9{,}520\times$ excess clustering over the ER baseline is exactly what one expects from a sub-critical FoF: galaxy groups are near-complete subgraphs (cliques) embedded in a sparse background graph. Their internal edges form triangles freely, while the background is connected only by narrow chains. This topological signature is the microscopic counterpart of the macroscopic sub-critical regime identified by the percolation scan.

\section{Conclusion}

We have shown that the maximum fragmentation criterion provides a self-consistent, unsupervised estimate of the galaxy group scale in a redshift-space spectroscopic survey. Applied to the SDSS DR10 volume-limited sample, the method recovers 98\% of Tempel et al.'s group memberships with an ARI of 0.807 and NMI of 0.983, without using any of their calibration inputs. The linking length emerges from the network itself, not from prior knowledge of what the answer should be.

The percolation framework offers two assets beyond the group catalog. First, the susceptibility peak $r_{\rm critical}$ provides a principled upper bound on the group scale and simultaneously identifies the cluster/supercluster regime, placing the full hierarchy of large-scale structure in a single topological picture. Second, the sub-critical ratio $r_{\rm groups}/r_{\rm critical} \approx 0.40$ is a measurable quantity that characterises the network's position in the phase diagram, comparable across surveys and redshifts.

The primary limitation is the fixed stretch factor $S = 10$. Its extension to a jointly optimised 2D scan over $(r_\perp, S)$ would remove the last external parameter and complete the unsupervised method. A second natural extension is a redshift-dependent $r_\perp$, necessary for deeper surveys where the mean inter-galaxy spacing varies significantly with depth.

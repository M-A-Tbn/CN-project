\section{Introduction}

\rhostart{G}alaxy groups, gravitationally bound systems of $2$--$\sim\!100$ members,
are the most common environment for galaxies in the universe and the fundamental
node of the cosmic web \cite{tempel2014}. Their identification from spectroscopic
redshift surveys relies on the Friends-of-Friends (FoF) algorithm
\cite{huchra1982, davis1985}: link every pair of galaxies within a chosen linking
length $\ell$; the connected components of the resulting graph are the groups.
The method maps a physical criterion (spatial proximity) directly onto a
graph-theoretic object (connected components), yet its central parameter, the
dimensionless linking length $b = \ell / \bar{d}$, has traditionally required
calibration against N-body simulations \cite{berlind2002, eke2004} or adoption
from prior surveys \cite{tempel2014}.

The question driving this project is whether the galaxy spatial network itself
encodes the group scale: can a purely topological analysis of the graph, without
external calibration, identify the physically correct linking length?

Percolation theory is the natural framework for this purpose \cite{newman2010,
stauffer1994}. In a random geometric graph, connectivity transitions sharply at a
critical linking length $r_c$: below $r_c$ the network fragments into isolated
finite clusters; above $r_c$ a single giant connected component (GCC) percolates
across the system. The susceptibility $\chi$ (the mean-square size of finite
components, excluding the GCC) diverges at $r_c$ and is the order parameter of
the transition. Below $r_c$, the finite components are precisely the structures we
call galaxy groups. Above $r_c$, they merge into clusters, filaments, and
superclusters.

Two topological observables are therefore available as unsupervised estimators of
the group scale. The susceptibility peak locates the percolation threshold
$r_{\rm critical}$, the hard upper bound above which group finding is no longer
meaningful. The fragmentation count $N_{\rm groups}(r_\perp)$ (the number of
finite components in the sub-GCC size range) peaks at the linking length at which
the network is most fragmented into distinct, finite groups. We identify this peak
as the group scale $r_{\rm groups}$. The sub-critical inequality
\begin{equation}
    r_{\rm groups} < r_{\rm critical}
\end{equation}
\medskip
is the defining property of the method and must be verified explicitly for every
sample.

Redshift-space distortions require an anisotropic (cylindrical) FoF. Virialized
galaxies have peculiar velocities of $\sim 300$--$500$ km/s that displace them by
several Mpc along the line of sight (Fingers of God effect), while their true
transverse extent is $\sim 0.5\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. We follow Tempel et al.\
\cite{tempel2014} in using a fixed stretch factor $S = r_\parallel / r_\perp = 10$.

Throughout this work, we use the Tempel et al.\ (2014) SDSS DR10 group catalog
\cite{tempel2014} as both the data source and the validation reference. Galaxy
positions, redshifts, and magnitudes come from their catalog; their group
assignments are the ground truth for validation. The linking length is derived
entirely from the topology of the network we construct; it is never read from
their paper.

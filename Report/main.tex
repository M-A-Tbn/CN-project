\begin{abstract}
We identify galaxy groups in the SDSS DR10 Main Galaxy Sample using a cylindrical
Friends-of-Friends algorithm, treating it explicitly as a random geometric graph
in comoving space. We derive the linking length from the network's own
percolation behaviour rather than calibrating it externally: the susceptibility peaks
at $r_{\mathrm{critical}} = 1.34\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, while the finite-group count peaks
at the sub-critical scale $r_{\mathrm{groups}} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, which we take
as the group-finding scale. Compared against the Tempel et al.\ catalogue as a
graph-partition problem, the groups score ARI $= 0.82$ and NMI $= 0.98$
(significant against a permutation null; $z \approx 1.4\times10^4$). The asymmetry
(high recall, lower precision) points to over-merging concentrated in the richest systems. The network is
itself far from random: clustering four orders of magnitude above the
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi baseline, and a degree distribution heavy-tailed at both ends.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{galaxy groups, Friends-of-Friends, network percolation, SDSS, large-scale structure, complex networks}

\section{Introduction}

\rhostart{G}alaxy groups, gravitationally bound systems of a few to a hundred
members, are the environment of most galaxies and the building blocks of the
cosmic web \cite{tempel2014}. Identifying them from a redshift survey is, at
heart, a graph problem: place an edge between every pair of galaxies closer than a
chosen linking length, and read the groups off as the connected components. This
Friends-of-Friends (FoF) prescription \cite{huchra1982, davis1985} maps a
physical criterion, spatial proximity, directly onto a graph-theoretic
object, but it leaves one parameter undetermined: the linking length itself,
conventionally written as the dimensionless $b = \ell/\bar d$. Traditionally $b$
has been fixed by calibration against $N$-body simulations \cite{berlind2002}
or adopted wholesale from an earlier survey \cite{tempel2014}.

This work asks whether the galaxy network can supply that scale itself. We treat
the FoF construction as a random geometric graph and study how its connectivity
changes as the linking length is varied. Such a graph undergoes a percolation
transition: below a critical linking length the network consists of small finite
clusters; above it a single giant component spans the volume.
Two scales come out of the transition. The susceptibility, the second moment of
the finite-cluster size distribution, peaks at the percolation threshold
$r_{\mathrm{critical}}$, above which only superclusters and filaments survive.
At a shorter scale, $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$, the number of distinct finite groups
reaches its maximum, just before inter-group bridges begin to dominate and
components merge into the giant. We adopt $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$ as the operating
point and require $r_{\mathrm{groups}} < r_{\mathrm{critical}}$.

Because the line-of-sight coordinate is reconstructed from redshift, peculiar
velocities stretch groups into radial filaments (the Fingers-of-God effect),
so the linking volume must be an anisotropic cylinder rather than a sphere
\cite{tempel2014}. Both scales are read off the topology; no external calibration sets $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$.

We apply the method to the SDSS DR10 Main Galaxy Sample and validate it against
the group catalogue of Tempel et al.\ \cite{tempel2014}. That catalogue has a
dual role: it provides the galaxy data, and its group assignments are the
ground truth against which our partition is measured. The linking length is read only from the network, never from Tempel's groups;
the comparison is framed as a graph-partition problem throughout.

\section{Data}

Deriving the linking length from network topology only works if the underlying
point set is unbiased: a distance-dependent selection would shift the percolation
scales by construction, so the sample below is cut to be volume-limited.

\subsection{Source catalog}

The galaxy sample is drawn from the catalogue of Tempel et al.\
\cite{tempel2014}, retrieved through the VizieR service. The underlying data are
the spectroscopic galaxies of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey Data Release 10 (SDSS
DR10) Main Galaxy Sample (MGS) \cite{ahn2014}. Spectroscopic redshifts are
precise enough that the dominant line-of-sight distortion is peculiar-velocity
stretching rather than measurement noise; that is what makes a redshift-space
group finder viable here. The MGS is magnitude-limited, complete to an apparent
$r$-band magnitude $m_{\mathrm{lim}} = 17.77$. From the catalogue we retain the
position, redshift and magnitude of each galaxy; the group identifiers assigned
by Tempel et al.\ are held back and used only as the validation ground truth
below.

\subsection{Cosmology}

Redshifts are converted to comoving distances under a flat $\Lambda$CDM cosmology
with $H_0 = 100\,h\,\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ and $\Omega_m = 0.27$. The
comoving distance to redshift $z$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:comoving}
    d_C(z) = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^z
    \frac{\mathrm{d}z'}{\sqrt{\Omega_m(1+z')^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}},
\end{equation}
with $\Omega_\Lambda = 1 - \Omega_m$. Setting $H_0 = 100\,h$ expresses every
distance in $h^{-1}$ Mpc, which sidesteps the uncertainty in the Hubble
constant. These are the same parameters Tempel et al.\ \cite{tempel2014} adopt;
a different cosmology would rescale all physical distances and make the
validation comparison meaningless.

\subsection{Volume-limited sample}

The MGS is flux-limited, so at increasing distance only progressively brighter
galaxies remain above $m_{\mathrm{lim}}$; a raw redshift slice would bias the
inferred group population toward more luminous systems at the far edge. We
restrict to $0.02 \le z \le 0.06$, where the lower bound avoids the regime where
peculiar-velocity errors dominate the redshift and the upper bound keeps the
volume compact enough for a single fixed linking length. We then impose the
absolute-magnitude limit corresponding to $m_{\mathrm{lim}}$ at the far edge,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Mlim}
    M_{\mathrm{lim}} = m_{\mathrm{lim}} - \mu(z_{\max}),
\end{equation}
where $\mu(z)$ is the distance modulus, and keep only galaxies with $M \le
M_{\mathrm{lim}}$. The sample is then complete to the same absolute magnitude
throughout the volume. No K-correction or extinction correction is applied; at
the low redshifts considered ($z \le 0.06$) these corrections are small, so the
incompleteness they introduce at the faint end is treated as a known limitation
rather than a source of systematic bias. These cuts reduce the parent catalogue
from $588{,}193$ to $75{,}042$ galaxies.

\subsection{Coordinate conversion}

Each galaxy is mapped from its observed $(d_C, \alpha, \delta)$ to Cartesian
coordinates,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cartesian}
    X = d_C\cos\delta\cos\alpha, \quad
    Y = d_C\cos\delta\sin\alpha, \quad
    Z = d_C\sin\delta,
\end{equation}
with the observer at the origin. The Cartesian embedding is needed both for the
graph construction and for decomposing pair separations into transverse and
line-of-sight components.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Cylindrical linking criterion}

The Friends-of-Friends procedure constructs a graph $G(r_\perp)$ on the set of
$N$ galaxies: each galaxy is a node, and an edge is drawn between every pair
whose separation falls below a chosen linking length. The connected components
of $G$ are identified as galaxy groups. This is the construction of a
\emph{random geometric graph} (RGG), here on the clustered galaxy distribution
rather than the uniform point process of the textbook model: the nodes carry
positions in a metric space (here the comoving Cartesian coordinates of each
galaxy), and adjacency is determined solely by proximity in that space. The group
membership that results is therefore derived entirely from the network structure,
with no reference to any pre-existing catalogue or external labelling. The procedure is in this sense unsupervised. Although the linking parameters must account for
redshift-space geometry (as described below), no group assignments from Tempel or
any other catalogue are used as input; those catalogues serve only as verification
after the fact.

A spherical linking length is, however, inappropriate in redshift space. The
line-of-sight coordinate is reconstructed from the observed redshift, which
mixes the cosmological recession with the peculiar velocity of each galaxy.
Within a virialised system, peculiar velocities of $300$--$500\,\mathrm{km\,
s^{-1}}$ displace galaxies by several $h^{-1}$ Mpc along the line of sight, while
the true transverse extent of the same system is only $\sim 0.5\,h^{-1}$ Mpc.
Groups are thereby stretched into radial filaments pointing at the observer,
the \emph{Fingers-of-God} effect \cite{jackson1972}. A spherical criterion large
enough to absorb this elongation would over-link in the transverse direction and
merge physically distinct systems.

The usual fix is an anisotropic, cylindrical linking volume
\cite{tempel2014}. For a pair $(i,j)$ with comoving positions
$\vec r_i, \vec r_j$, the separation is decomposed along the line of sight. With
the observer at the origin, the local line-of-sight direction at the pair
midpoint is $\hat{\ell}_{ij} = (\vec r_i + \vec r_j)/|\vec r_i + \vec r_j|$, and
the parallel and perpendicular separations are
\begin{equation}\label{eq:decomp}
    d_\parallel = \bigl| (\vec r_i - \vec r_j)\cdot \hat{\ell}_{ij}\bigr|,
    \qquad
    d_\perp = \sqrt{\,|\vec r_i - \vec r_j|^2 - d_\parallel^2\,}.
\end{equation}
The pair is linked when it lies inside a cylinder aligned with the line of sight,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cylinder}
    d_\perp \le r_\perp
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    d_\parallel \le r_\parallel = S\,r_\perp,
\end{equation}
where $r_\perp$ is the transverse linking length and $S$ the dimensionless
stretch factor. Following Tempel et al.\ \cite{tempel2014} we fix $S = 10$; this
is a shape parameter set by the typical ratio of radial to transverse
distortion, and it is the only parameter taken from the literature.
The scale $r_\perp$ is left free and is fixed entirely by the topological
analysis below. Candidate pairs are found with a $k$-d tree: a
single range query returns every pair inside the bounding sphere of radius
$r_{\max} = \sqrt{r_\perp^2 + r_\parallel^2}$ that encloses the cylinder, after
which \equref{eq:cylinder} selects the true links.
\figref{fig:cylinder} illustrates the two linking volumes.

\subsection{Percolation scan}

The transverse linking length is the single free parameter of the construction,
and the structure of the graph changes qualitatively as it is varied. We scan $r_\perp$ over $[0.1, 2.5)\,h^{-1}$ Mpc in steps of
$0.02\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. The lower bound is set well below the expected group scale
(${\sim}0.5\,h^{-1}$ Mpc) to capture the onset of the percolation transition
from below; the upper bound is set above $r_{\mathrm{critical}}$ to confirm that
the GCC is fully established. The step size of $0.02\,h^{-1}$ Mpc gives
sufficient resolution to localise both peaks to better than one step. The graph
$G(r_\perp)$ is rebuilt and its connected components extracted at each step, and
we monitor two complementary topological observables.

The first is the \emph{susceptibility},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:chi}
    \chi(r_\perp) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{s\,\neq\,\mathrm{GCC}} s^2\, n_s,
\end{equation}
where $n_s$ is the number of components containing $s$ galaxies and the sum runs
over every component except the giant connected component (GCC). The
susceptibility is proportional to the second moment of the finite-cluster size
distribution; in the thermodynamic limit it diverges at the percolation
threshold, and for a finite system it peaks there. We denote this peak location $r_{\mathrm{critical}}$. It
is the scale at which a single component first spans the survey volume: above it
the analysis recovers filaments and superclusters rather than groups.

The second observable is the \emph{fragmentation count},
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nfrag}
    N_{\mathrm{groups}}(r_\perp)
    = \bigl|\{\,c : 2 \le |c| < |\mathrm{GCC}|\,\}\bigr|,
\end{equation}
the number of components that are neither isolated nodes nor the GCC. Galaxy
groups sit below the percolation threshold: too small to register in the
susceptibility peak, yet physically real. A linking length that is too short
leaves them as unresolved singletons; one that is too long chains them into
the percolating component. At intermediate lengths the network resolves into the
largest number of distinct finite systems, and that maximum is the criterion used
to select $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$, with no reference to any external catalogue.
Tempel's catalogue enters only afterwards, as a check on whether the
topology-derived groups correspond to physically recognised structures.

\subsection{GCC diagnostic}

\figref{fig:gcc} shows both scales directly. The upper panel tracks the
fraction of galaxies in the GCC as $r_\perp$ increases; the lower panel shows
its derivative. Three independent
markers of the transition are overlaid. The susceptibility peak places $r_{\mathrm{critical}} = 1.34\,h^{-1}$ Mpc.
The maximum-gradient point gives $1.36\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, in close agreement.
The fragmentation maximum sits well below both, at $r_{\mathrm{groups}} =
0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. The defining requirement of the method,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:subcritical}
    r_{\mathrm{groups}} < r_{\mathrm{critical}},
\end{equation}
is therefore satisfied: the group-finding scale is well below the percolation
threshold. $r_{\mathrm{critical}}$ is not used to find groups; it is computed
to confirm that $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$ sits in the sub-critical regime where the
network still resolves distinct finite structures.

\subsection{Linking parameter}

To connect the topologically derived scale to the conventional FoF literature,
we express it as the dimensionless linking parameter
\begin{equation}\label{eq:b}
    b \equiv \frac{r_{\mathrm{groups}}}{\bar d},
    \qquad \bar d = n^{-1/3},
    \qquad n = \frac{N}{V},
\end{equation}
where $\bar d$ is the mean inter-galaxy separation and $n$ the number density of
the sample. The volume-limited slice occupies a spherical shell between the
comoving distances at $z_{\min}$ and $z_{\max}$, restricted to the survey
footprint,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:vol}
    V = \frac{4\pi}{3}\bigl(d_{\max}^3 - d_{\min}^3\bigr)\,f_{\mathrm{sky}},
    \qquad
    f_{\mathrm{sky}} = \frac{7221\,\mathrm{deg}^2}{41253\,\mathrm{deg}^2},
\end{equation}
the SDSS Main Galaxy Sample covering approximately $7221\,\mathrm{deg}^2$.
This approximates the survey volume as a uniform spherical shell, without
accounting for the detailed angular mask or boundary holes; the resulting $b$
is therefore indicative rather than precise. This gives $b = 0.144$, near the lower edge of the classical calibrated range $0.14 < b < 0.20$
\cite{davis1985, berlind2002} and slightly above the redshift-dependent Tempel
et al.\ value ($b \approx 0.113$--$0.115$ over the same interval). The
computation of $b$ is purely for comparison with the literature.

\subsection{Final network}

With $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$ fixed, the group catalogue is produced by building the graph once at the operating
point $r_{\mathrm{groups}} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc. The resulting network contains
$N = 75{,}042$ nodes and $95{,}310$ edges. Its connected components are extracted
and each galaxy is assigned a group label; this output is exactly that of
single-linkage hierarchical clustering cut at $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$, the simplest
community-detection rule, in which two galaxies share a group whenever they are
joined by a chain of pairwise links. It imposes no resolution limit, but is correspondingly sensitive to bridging:
a single close pair can merge two otherwise distinct systems. Both are taken up in the next section.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The percolation transition}

\figref{fig:phase} shows both observables across the full scan. The susceptibility
peak at $r_{\mathrm{critical}} = 1.34\,h^{-1}$ Mpc and the fragmentation maximum
at $r_{\mathrm{groups}} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc are both sharp and well-resolved,
consistent with the two scales located in \figref{fig:gcc}.

The transition is the spatial-graph counterpart of the giant-component transition
in random-graph theory. In an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph the giant component emerges
at mean degree $\langle k\rangle = 1$, that is, at edge probability $p_c = 1/N$;
in a random geometric graph the giant component instead appears at a constant
critical mean degree (continuum percolation), independent of $N$; the
$(\ln N / N)^{1/d}$ radius often quoted for geometric graphs is the stricter
full-connectivity threshold, not the percolation onset. At our operating point the mean
degree is $\langle k\rangle = 2.54$, well above the threshold at which an equivalent
random graph would develop a giant component, yet no comparable giant component
appears in the FoF network. Groups assemble locally long before anything percolates globally; the mean-field estimate below
makes the gap quantitative.

The sharpness of the peak is itself a finite-size effect. A true divergence
occurs only in the thermodynamic limit; for a finite graph the transition is a
smooth crossover whose width narrows as $N$ grows. The
volume-limited sample of $\sim 7.5\times10^4$ galaxies, drawn from a parent
catalogue of $\sim 6\times10^5$, is large enough that the peak is well localised and $r_{\mathrm{critical}}$
can be identified with good precision, as confirmed by the close agreement
between the susceptibility peak and the maximum-gradient cross-check.

The mean-field theory of percolation provides an analytic check on these
numbers. In an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi graph, the probability $u$ that a randomly
chosen node lies outside the giant component satisfies the self-consistency
equation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:gcc_mf}
    u = e^{\lambda(u-1)},
\end{equation}
where $\lambda = \langle k\rangle$. A nontrivial solution ($u < 1$, giant
component present) exists only for $\lambda > 1$, recovering the mean-field
threshold at $\langle k\rangle = 1$. At the operating point $\lambda = 2.54$,
the equation gives $u \approx 0.10$: a random graph of the same edge density
would place $90\%$ of its nodes in a single giant component, more than a
hundred times the $0.8\%$ observed in the FoF network. Spatial embedding in the cosmic web suppresses the global percolation that
mean-field theory predicts at this mean degree; we attribute this to the galaxy
distribution being too inhomogeneous for a spanning component to assemble at
the scale where groups form.

The sky projections provide a first check that both catalogues trace the same large-scale structure before any quantitative comparison.

detected catalogue (right column) onto the plane of the sky in four redshift bins
of width $\Delta z = 0.01$ (rows). Grouped galaxies are coloured by their group

projection, which collapses the line of sight, the two catalogues show closely
matching large-scale structure: the same sky overdensities appear grouped in both
columns. Because the line of sight is collapsed, the projection cannot establish
3D group identity; that is the role of the quantitative metrics in
\S\ref{sec:validation}. The number of grouped galaxies per bin tracks the underlying galaxy count with
no systematic growth with redshift, consistent with the fixed transverse linking
length remaining appropriate across the volume-limited range.

angular-to-Cartesian transformation or the comoving-distance integral would show
up as spatial offsets between matched groups in the two columns, and none appear.
At larger scales, prominent overdensities thread through adjacent redshift bins,
and both catalogues assign them to the same structures: the cylindrical FoF
follows extended associations without breaking them at the panel boundaries.

\section{Validation \& Assessment}\label{sec:validation}

Validating the catalogue against Tempel et al.\ is a \emph{graph-partition
comparison}: both catalogues partition the same set of galaxies into groups, and
the question is how closely the two partitions agree. We address it at three
levels: individual matched groups (by richness and by redshift), the global
partition, and the network topology (clustering and degree distribution). The
section then profiles the richest components individually.

\needspace{4\baselineskip}
\subsection{Completeness and purity by richness}

At the group level, for each Tempel group $T$ (with at least two members) we identify the detected
group $D$ with which it shares the most galaxies and compute
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cp}
    \mathrm{Completeness} = \frac{|T \cap D|}{|T|}, \qquad
    \mathrm{Purity} = \frac{|T \cap D|}{|D|}.
\end{equation}
The two are asymmetric: completeness is the fraction of a reference group
recovered, purity the fraction of the matched detected group that is genuine.
Binned by richness (\tabref{tab:richness}), completeness is uniformly high
($\gtrsim 0.98$) and shows no trend: essentially every member of a Tempel group
is recovered. Purity instead declines monotonically, from $0.82$ for pairs to
$0.75$ for the richest systems. The detected groups thus contain all the true
members but, in the richest systems, a few additional galaxies. Rich groups sit in the densest environments, with a single fixed linking volume
most easily bridging to neighbouring galaxies and structures; the declining
purity reflects the fixed aperture bridging into neighbouring galaxies at high
density, not a failure to span the internal velocity dispersion
(which would depress completeness, not purity).

\subsection{Completeness and purity by redshift}

\figref{fig:cpz} repeats the matched-group analysis binned by mean group
redshift. Completeness remains near unity (consistent with the global value of $0.98$
in \tabref{tab:metrics}) and purity fluctuates around its mean of $\approx 0.79$
with no systematic trend across redshift. The fixed linking length
therefore does not over- or under-link with distance over the volume-limited
range, consistent with the sky projection of \figref{fig:radec}.

The stability is physically expected: $r_\perp$ is applied in comoving
coordinates, so the physical scale probed does not drift with distance, and the
volume-limited cut suppresses the Malmquist-type bias that would otherwise
inflate group sizes at the far edge of the survey.

\subsection{Global partition metrics}

For the global comparison, the two partitions are evaluated on the $41{,}430$
galaxies that Tempel places in groups of at least two members ($10{,}907$ Tempel
groups, $9{,}739$ detected groups). The remaining $75{,}042 - 41{,}430 =
33{,}612$ galaxies are those Tempel classifies as field objects. This set is not
homogeneous: it contains galaxies that our method also leaves isolated (genuine
agreement), but may also contain galaxies that our method links into a detected
group (a false positive invisible to the metrics below). Restricting the
comparison to the Tempel-grouped subsample therefore gives a conservative
evaluation of completeness but may slightly overstate pair precision, since
false positives drawn from the Tempel field are not penalised. \tabref{tab:metrics} reports two standard partition-comparison scores. The
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) measures the fraction of pairs on which both
partitions agree (whether by placing them in the same group or in different
groups), corrected for chance; the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) measures
the information one partition carries about the other. Both lie on $[0,1]$. The
detected partition scores ARI $= 0.82$ and NMI $= 0.98$.

The pair confusion matrix makes the asymmetry concrete. Pair recall, the fraction
of Tempel co-group pairs recovered, is $0.98$, while pair precision, the fraction
of our co-group pairs that are genuine, is $0.71$. At the level of pairs this
repeats the richness-analysis conclusion: the catalogue is highly complete but
over-merged in the richest systems. We also test whether the agreement is significant. Permuting
the detected labels $1000$ times and recomputing the ARI produces a null
distribution centred at zero with standard deviation $5.7\times10^{-5}$; the
observed value lies far outside the null ($z \approx 1.4\times10^4$);
the agreement is not a chance artefact.

\needspace{6\baselineskip}
\subsection{Network topology}

At $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$ the full network has $75{,}042$ nodes and $95{,}310$
edges, fragmenting into $37{,}500$ connected components; the largest spans only $585$ nodes ($0.8\%$),
consistent with the sub-critical operating point. The mean degree is $\langle k\rangle = 2.54$.
The Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi null (a random graph with the same number of nodes and
edges, hence edge probability $p_{\mathrm{ER}} = 2E/[N(N-1)] = 3.4\times10^{-5}$)
has an expected clustering coefficient equal to $p_{\mathrm{ER}}$. The measured
transitivity, $0.646$ (\tabref{tab:clustering}), exceeds this by four orders of
magnitude. That gap is real but weak on its own: any graph built from spatial
proximity is locally clustered, so a structureless null is beaten before any
cosmology enters.

The sharper test is a \emph{geometric} null: $75{,}042$ points placed uniformly
within the survey footprint (RA--Dec positions drawn within the occupied
one-degree pixel mask, comoving distances drawn uniformly in volume), linked by
the same cylindrical criterion with the linking length bisected to match the real
mean degree $\langle k\rangle = 2.54$. Averaged over eight independent
realisations this null has transitivity $0.45$ and mean local clustering $0.32$
(\tabref{tab:clustering}). The galaxy network matches it in mean local clustering
($0.32$) but exceeds it in transitivity ($0.646$ against $0.45$, a factor of
$1.4$ and far outside the realisation scatter of $\pm 0.002$). The excess is confined to the high-degree, dense environments that dominate
transitivity, the rich groups and cluster cores, consistent with gravitational
clustering having drawn galaxies into extra closed triangles. The same clustering shows
in the linking lengths: the real network reaches $\langle k\rangle = 2.54$ at
$0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, less than half the $\approx 1.3\,h^{-1}$ Mpc the uniform null
needs for the same mean degree.

\subsection{Degree distribution}

\figref{fig:degree} shows the degree distribution $P(k)$ of the same network on
semi-logarithmic and double-logarithmic axes, with the Poisson law of the
Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi null at the same mean degree overlaid. The distribution
departs from the null at both extremes. The isolated-node fraction is $34.9\%$
(field galaxies of degree zero), against a Poisson expectation of $7.9\%$,
an excess of $4.4\times$. At the opposite end a heavy tail reaches $k_{\max} =
43$, populated by hub galaxies whose neighbourhoods a Poisson graph of the same
mean degree would essentially never produce; the null lies tens of orders of
magnitude below the data at high $k$. Because probability is conserved, the
over-population of both extremes leaves the distribution depleted near its mean:
galaxies are either isolated in the field or embedded in a dense group, with few
in between. The hubs are the most connected, central members of the richest groups.

The double-logarithmic panel shows the tail is not a pure power law: it is
concave and truncated at $k_{\max}$. The cutoff arises because the degree is bounded by the number of galaxies that
can occupy a single linking cylinder; finite sample size and luminosity selection
also contribute, but the geometric constraint is the dominant factor at the
linking length used here. The network is therefore heavy-tailed but not scale-free,
consistent with a random geometric graph rather than a preferential-attachment
network.

\subsection{Internal structure of the richest groups}

The global metrics in \S\ref{sec:validation} are computed on the $9{,}739$ detected groups; \tabref{tab:groups}
profiles the eight richest of these individually. The eight groups show a consistent pattern: the internal edge density rises as groups shrink, from $0.02$ for the
largest to $0.08$ for the smallest tabulated. The transitivity stays in the
range $0.54$–$0.64$ throughout. Yet even the richest groups are far from compact: diameters reaching $16$ to
$36$ links across only a few hundred members indicate elongated, sparsely
connected structures rather than clique-like balls. Single-linkage clustering produces exactly such structures, a
direct manifestation of its bridging sensitivity. The giant connected component
($585$ members, diameter $22$) is the largest such chain; group $1017$, with a
diameter of $36$ across $226$ members, is the most filamentary of all.

The same bridging sensitivity that produces filamentary internal structure is
what drives the over-merging seen in \tabref{tab:richness} and \tabref{tab:metrics}.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The main result is that the group scale can be recovered from the network
alone. The fragmentation criterion selects $r_{\mathrm{groups}} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$
Mpc, or $b = 0.144$, with no input from the reference catalogue, and the
resulting groups reproduce the Tempel partition at ARI $= 0.82$. The
topologically chosen linking length is slightly larger than Tempel's effective $b
\approx 0.113$–$0.115$, and this is consistent with the validation: a marginally longer linking length explains the over-merging
seen as declining purity in rich systems (\tabref{tab:richness}) and as pair
precision below recall (\tabref{tab:metrics}). The fragmentation maximum favours
linking lengths just large enough to assemble groups fully, at the cost of
occasionally bridging close pairs, the characteristic weakness of single
linkage.

The percolation scan also converts a free parameter into a measured one. The
susceptibility and fragmentation curves give two physically motivated scales:
$r_{\mathrm{critical}}$, above which group finding recovers filaments rather than
groups, and $r_{\mathrm{groups}}$, at which the group population is largest. The
sub-critical inequality $r_{\mathrm{groups}} < r_{\mathrm{critical}}$ is an
internal consistency check that any FoF catalogue should satisfy.

The method has known failure modes. Single-linkage clustering has no resolution
limit but is sensitive to bridging: a single close pair merges two groups and
produces the extended, filamentary components of \tabref{tab:groups} and the
over-merging in rich systems. The fixed stretch factor $S = 10$ cannot adapt to the
full range of cluster velocity dispersions either; we have not scanned $S$, so its
separate contribution to the over-merging is not isolated here. A single transverse linking length is held across the full volume. The
redshift-binned metrics show this is adequate over $0.02 \le z \le 0.06$, but
a deeper sample would need a redshift-dependent length. The survey footprint also truncates groups at its boundary, an edge
effect not corrected here.

More broadly, the fact that $b = 0.144$ lands near the lower edge of the classical calibrated range
$0.14$--$0.20$ \cite{davis1985, berlind2002} without any external calibration
input suggests that the percolation transition tracks something physical about
the clustering scale, rather than an artefact of how the scan was set up.

\section{Conclusion}

The central question was whether the linking length for a galaxy group finder
can be fixed by the network's own topology rather than by calibration. The
fragmentation maximum at $r_{\mathrm{groups}} = 0.54\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, read from
a percolation scan with no reference to any external catalogue, reproduces the
Tempel et al.\ partition at ARI $= 0.82$ and NMI $= 0.98$. The residual
disagreement is over-merging in the richest systems, attributable to
single-linkage bridging and the fixed stretch factor rather than to a problem
with the percolation criterion. That $b = 0.144$ falls near the lower edge of the classical
calibrated range without being calibrated there suggests that topology alone is sufficient to identify the operating scale, independent of any external reference catalogue.
